TI-84 Probability: Binomial Distributions Made Simple

A binomial distribution describes the number of successes in a fixed number of independent trials, when each trial has the same probability of success. It is one of the most useful ideas in introductory statistics, because so many real questions fit this pattern: how many free throws will a player make, how many survey respondents will say yes, or how many items in a sample will be defective?

On a TI-84, two built-in functions do almost all of the work. Use binompdf when you want the probability of exactly a certain number of successes, and use binomcdf when you want the probability of up to a certain number of successes. Both live in the DISTR menu: press 2nd, then VARS, and scroll to binompdf or binomcdf.

How to use binompdf

The syntax is binompdf(n, p, x), where n is the number of trials, p is the probability of success on one trial, and x is the exact number of successes you want. For example, binompdf(10, 0.3, 4) gives the probability of exactly four successes in ten trials when each trial has a 30% chance of success.

How to use binomcdf

The syntax is binomcdf(n, p, x), and it adds up the probabilities for 0, 1, 2, and so on up to x successes. So binomcdf(10, 0.3, 4) is the probability of four or fewer successes. To find the probability of at least five successes, use the complement: 1 - binomcdf(10, 0.3, 4).

Worked example

Suppose a student guesses on a ten-question quiz, with four choices per question, so the chance of guessing one question correctly is 0.25. What is the probability of getting exactly three right? Enter binompdf(10, 0.25, 3) and the calculator returns about 0.2503, or roughly 25%. The probability of getting three or fewer right is binomcdf(10, 0.25, 3), about 0.7759. Notice how the second answer is larger: it includes the chances of getting zero, one, two, or three questions right.

Before you trust an answer, check that your problem really is binomial: the number of trials must be fixed, each trial must have only two outcomes, the trials must be independent, and the probability of success must stay the same. If any of those conditions fails, a different distribution may be needed.
